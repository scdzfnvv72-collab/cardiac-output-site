It's two in the morning. Your patient had an aortic valve replacement this afternoon, good ventricle, off bypass without a fuss, and the noradrenaline has crept up again. Your general intensive care instinct says work out why they're vasoplegic and turn the pressors up. In this specific patient, that instinct is wrong. Nine times out of ten they aren't vasoplegic. They're empty.

Aortic stenosis and the problems it creates on the cardiac unit: the intensive care half. The cath lab half, on TAVI and sedation, is the next episode.

## Why that patient is empty

Aortic stenosis is mechanically an outflow tract obstruction, so the ventricle hypertrophies against it. This afternoon the surgeon fixed the obstruction, but the ventricle didn't get the memo. A thick, stiff, non-compliant ventricle is exquisitely preload-dependent, and reaching for the vasopressor instead of the fluid will have you chasing that patient all night. Expect to give five litres in twenty-four hours, expect them to be overloaded a few days later, and don't reach for albumin on day zero, because by the time it arrives your patient is another litre behind.

## The disease

Why symptoms are the prognostic trigger (a one-year mortality of fifty percent from the moment they appear), and the murky world of low-gradient aortic stenosis, where the gradient depends on a ventricle that can still generate it. The sickest patients are precisely the ones the measurement fails on, underestimating severity and overestimating valve area. The velocity ratio is dimensionless and sidesteps the problem. The four classes of severe aortic stenosis, dobutamine stress echo for pseudo-severe disease, and why gated CT with a calcium score has displaced echo as the most important scan for structural heart disease.

The heart team, and why anaesthetists and intensivists belong on it: we're generalists, and we're good at assessing risk, where once we were simply the brake at the end of the process.

## Two things that land on the reg overnight

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest: the principles are the same wherever the patient is, and the cardinal rule is not to prognosticate early, because the picture genuinely changes and the story matters as much as the tests. And pacing: AAI is fine after grafts but dangerous after valve surgery, VVI backup at 30–40 is your safety net, interrogate the box daily, restore community settings before discharge, and if a patient arrests, look at the pacing box before you open the chest.

## Key takeaways
- The post-AVR patient with a hypertrophied ventricle is preload-dependent: when the pressure sags they're usually empty, not vasoplegic
- Five litres in 24 hours is normal here; expect overload and diuretics a few days later
- Don't use albumin on day-zero hearts: it arrives too late to help, and there's no evidence one fluid beats another
- Symptoms are the trigger in aortic stenosis: one-year mortality of 50% from the moment they appear
- In low-gradient disease the gradient lies: it underestimates severity and overestimates valve area
- The velocity ratio is dimensionless, so it sidesteps the geometric assumptions and the need for a high gradient
- Gated CT with a calcium score has displaced echo as the key scan in structural heart disease
- Anaesthetists and intensivists belong on the heart team because we're generalists and we assess risk
- Never prognosticate early after an out-of-hospital arrest: go multimodal, and weigh the story alongside the tests
- AAI is fine after grafts but dangerous after valve surgery; VVI backup at 30–40 is the safety net
- If a patient arrests on the unit, look at the pacing box first

## Questions
::: qa
Q: Why is AAI pacing acceptable after CABG but risky after aortic valve replacement?
A: Because valve surgery near the conduction system carries a real risk of complete heart block, and AAI depends on intact AV conduction; if the node fails, there is no ventricular backup.
R: Debridement of a calcified aortic annulus or sutures in the membranous septum can injure the AV node or bundle. After valve surgery use DDD, or at minimum a VVI backup at 30–40, and interrogate the box daily.

Q: A patient with an ejection fraction of 25% has a mean gradient of 22 mmHg and a valve area of 0.8 cm². Is the stenosis severe?
A: Possibly. This is low-flow, low-gradient aortic stenosis, where the gradient underestimates severity because the ventricle cannot generate it. Use the velocity ratio, a calcium score, or dobutamine stress echo to decide.
R: Gradient is proportional to flow squared, so a failing ventricle produces a small gradient across a severely stenotic valve. Dobutamine distinguishes true severe (area stays small as flow rises) from pseudo-severe (area opens up). The CT calcium score is flow-independent.

Q: Why does a hypertrophied ventricle after AVR need so much fluid?
A: A thick, non-compliant ventricle has a steep diastolic pressure-volume relationship: small reductions in preload produce large falls in stroke volume, and the hypertrophy persists for months after the obstruction is removed.
R: Vasodilation from rewarming and anaesthesia unmasks the preload dependence. Vasopressors alone raise afterload on a ventricle that is already empty; fluid restores the stroke volume. The bill comes a few days later as overload and diuretics.
:::
