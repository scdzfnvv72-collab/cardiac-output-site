It's three in the morning. Your patient is proned, paralysed, on 100% oxygen, and his P/F ratio is 70. Do you keep cranking the ventilator, or do you pick up the phone?

This episode works through veno-venous ECMO the way it actually gets used on an ICU: what it does, who it's for, and the things that catch people out at the bedside.

## What ECMO is, and isn't

The single most important idea in ECMO is that it doesn't treat anything. It is a bridge, and that reframes every decision you make: no bridgeable destination, no bridge. Then the physiology consultants love to quiz you on. Sweep gas controls CO₂. Blood flow controls oxygenation. VV-ECMO provides no haemodynamic support at all, so preserved cardiac function is a prerequisite, and a septic, hyperdynamic patient can desaturate with a perfectly functioning circuit because their native cardiac output is simply outrunning the flow you can deliver.

## Who qualifies

Candidacy uses the EOLIA and CESAR thresholds, the Murray score, and the harder question of whether the lungs have a plausible route back. Optimise first, and prone them, before you refer.

## The evidence, honestly

CESAR randomised to referral to an ECMO centre, not to ECMO. EOLIA was stopped for futility with an 11% mortality gap, and the 28% crossover in the control arm makes "negative trial" the wrong conclusion: the sickest control patients were rescued with ECMO and counted against it. The Bayesian re-analysis, the individual-patient-data meta-analysis and the COVID-era NHS England data all point the same way.

## The service, and the bedside

The UK has nationally commissioned centres (now including Barts, Bristol and Newcastle), and a referral that gets your patient assessed fast is one that arrives with the ventilator settings, gases, and the story. Then cannulation configurations, lung-rest ventilation, the bleeding-versus-clotting balancing act, and the rapid-fire troubleshooting round: recirculation, the suddenly desaturating patient, line chatter, and the failing oxygenator. Recirculation is the VV gremlin. Harlequin syndrome is a VA problem, not a VV one.

## Questions
::: qa
Q: A patient on VV-ECMO has an arterial saturation of 84%. The circuit is flowing at 4.5 L/min with a post-oxygenator saturation of 100%. What is the most likely explanation, and what would you not do?
A: The native cardiac output is high relative to circuit flow, so a large fraction of venous return bypasses the oxygenator. Do not turn up the sweep gas.
R: Oxygenation on VV depends on the ratio of circuit flow to cardiac output; sweep gas only changes CO₂ clearance. A hyperdynamic (often septic) patient can outrun the circuit. Options are to increase flow, reduce native cardiac output (sedation, treat the driver), accept a lower saturation, or check for recirculation, which is the other common cause.

Q: Why is "EOLIA was negative" the wrong conclusion?
A: Because 28% of the control arm crossed over to ECMO as rescue, and those patients were analysed in the control group.
R: Intention-to-treat protects randomisation, but when the sickest controls receive the intervention and survive, the control arm is flattered. The 11% absolute mortality difference did not reach significance in a trial stopped early for futility; the Bayesian re-analysis and the IPD meta-analysis with CESAR both suggest a benefit.

Q: What single fact about a referral most changes whether it is accepted?
A: Whether the lung disease has a plausible route to recovery within a survivable run length.
R: ECMO buys time; it cannot cure. A reversible cause (asthma, aspiration, influenza) with a short expected run is an easy yes even with alarming numbers; an irreversible process is a no even with a young patient.
:::
