It's the middle of the night, again. Your post-op cardiac patient looks awful, and the nurse says the cardiac output's low. Do you reach for a number, or do you look at the patient?

This is heart failure and mechanical circulatory support the way it actually plays out on a cardiothoracic ICU: how you know the output is low, what the devices really do, and how to escalate before it's too late. The theatre half is a separate episode.

## Cardiac output is a clinical diagnosis

Venous saturations and the Fick principle are clues rather than answers. The picture that actually tells you the pump is failing is the tachycardia they're leaning on, the deranged liver enzymes and kidneys, the rising lactate, and the low-output gut that gets mistaken for an acute abdomen. The pulmonary artery catheter is still the gold standard against a field of derived monitors, and floating one is a skill worth having, trace by trace.

## The machines

The single most important habit on the unit: on any patient on support, always work out where the cannulas are and where the blood is going. Use it to walk through VA-ECMO (bypassing the heart and lungs means stagnation, clot, and a patient you usually can't wake; the elegant fix is femoral drainage with an axillary return), BiVADs (both sides supported, awake for weeks), and the ambulatory LVAD, where flow is derived from power rather than measured, the pump is preload-dependent and afterload-sensitive, and the shocked HeartMate patient in the Emergency Department needs fluid before you pick up the phone.

## Escalation

Milrinone, then dopamine, then the balloon pump, and by then you're knocking on the door of a mechanical support assessment. Plus inhaled nitric, a monitoring trap with the axillary return line, an honest look at ECMO-CPR, and why Harlequin syndrome is the signature limitation of peripheral VA-ECMO.

## Key takeaways
- Cardiac output is a clinical diagnosis, not a number; venous sats and monitors are clues, not answers
- On any support device, always work out where the cannulas are and where the blood is flowing
- VA-ECMO bypasses heart and lungs: it clots, it's temporary, and you usually can't wake the patient; femoral drainage with an axillary return is the elegant fix
- LVAD flow is derived from power; it's preload-dependent and afterload-sensitive, so when in doubt, give fluid
- Milrinone → dopamine → balloon pump is the escalation ladder; a balloon pump means an MCS assessment is close
- Harlequin (differential hypoxaemia) is the signature limitation of peripheral VA-ECMO

## Questions
::: qa
Q: A HeartMate 3 patient arrives in the Emergency Department hypotensive with a low-flow alarm. What is the first intervention?
A: Give fluid.
R: A continuous-flow LVAD is preload-dependent: an empty left ventricle collapses around the inflow cannula (a suction event), flow falls, and the console shows low flow and low pulsatility. Vasopressors alone don't fix an empty pump. Fluid first, then look for the cause (bleeding, sepsis, RV failure, arrhythmia) and call the VAD centre.

Q: Why does Harlequin syndrome occur on peripheral VA-ECMO and not on VV?
A: Because on peripheral VA the oxygenated return enters the femoral artery and meets native ejection somewhere in the aorta; if the native lungs are poor, the heart and brain are perfused by deoxygenated blood from the left ventricle.
R: The mixing point moves distally as native cardiac output recovers. Measure the gas from the right radial artery, which reflects what the brain sees. On VV, all blood passes through the native lungs after the circuit, so there is no competing source.

Q: On a patient with an axillary return cannula, why can the right radial arterial trace mislead you?
A: The right radial artery is downstream of the axillary return, so it reads the circuit's pressure and oxygenation rather than the patient's.
R: Monitor from the left radial (or femoral), and be alert to hyperperfusion of the right arm.
:::
