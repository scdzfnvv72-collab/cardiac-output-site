Recorded minutes after the live launch at Annual Congress 2026, and published the same day the paper appeared in Anaesthesia. GLIMPSE (the GLP-1 receptor agonist Management in the Peri-operative Setting project) is a national, prospective, multicentre cohort study delivered through the UK resident research networks across all four nations, and it is the largest thing anyone has done on this question. We take it apart the way we did ITACS: the question, the methods, the results, the critique. Then the half that wasn't in the room.

Two numbers to start. One in 36 patients presenting for anaesthesia in the UK is taking a GLP-1 receptor agonist. And in those patients, the incidence of pulmonary aspiration or regurgitation was 1 in 71, against 1 in 802 in everyone else: an odds ratio of about 11. The rest of the episode is the work of deciding how much of that to believe.

## What the study was designed to answer

The framing that governs everything: the primary outcome is a prevalence, not a comparison. GLIMPSE was designed to answer "how many?" The aspiration finding is a secondary outcome, and every conclusion about the odds ratio has to sit inside that.

Each site chose its own fortnight and screened every patient's drug history. Twelve sites that screened fewer than three quarters of their eligible patients were deliberately discarded, which leaves a 97% screening rate and a remarkably complete national snapshot. The exclusions matter to us more than to most readers: patients whose airway management began outside the procedure location, ICU transfers explicitly, are not in the dataset.

## The prevalence findings

These are more interesting than the headline. Between institutions, use ranged from 0.4% to 7.8%, a twenty-fold difference, so the national average is close to useless for planning your own list. Tirzepatide accounts for 70% and semaglutide 26%, and this is now predominantly a weight-loss population rather than a diabetes one.

The number that should change practice this week: 41% obtained the drug from somewhere that is neither a GP nor a hospital specialist. It will not be on the GP summary, the discharge letter or the drug chart. It is only there if somebody asks the patient directly, by name.

## What clinicians did

Management was highly variable. Thirty per cent were asked to stop, most for 8–14 days, consistent with "stop it for a week", which the paper quietly demolishes: with half-lives of five and seven days, five half-lives means 25 and 35 days. A week does not eliminate the drug, and national consensus guidance says to continue it anyway. Meanwhile gastric ultrasound, the one bedside test that answers precisely the question being asked, was used in 1.3% of patients.

## The safety finding

Of the 19 events in the exposed group, all 19 involved regurgitation and only two reached the lung, which suggests that the intubation, head-up positioning and pre-oxygenation were doing their job. And the finding we would most like to change practice: more than half the events happened at emergence, not induction.

The critique covers association without any ability to adjust for confounding, observer bias inflating the ratio from both ends (with the NAP7 cross-check that partly defends it), small event numbers driving a large effect, and generalisability. The strengths get their due too.

## The cardiothoracic half

This is the part the paper does not address. Our prevalence is probably higher than the national figure. The months between assessment and surgery make the drug-history gap worse for us. Emergence happens on the intensive care unit, hours later, which makes this a handover problem as much as an airway problem. We put a probe into the stomach of nearly every patient we anaesthetise, so we already own the skill that was used in 1% of cases nationally.

Then the dilemmas: the double-lumen tube versus tube-exchange question, taken from a real case in the paper; the conflict between a rapid sequence induction and haemodynamic stability in critical aortic stenosis; and why stopping the drug costs our patients more, through hyperglycaemia and sternal wound infection, without reliably emptying the stomach.

## Key takeaways
- 1 in 36 patients presenting for anaesthesia in the UK is taking a GLP-1 receptor agonist, but between hospitals the rate ranged from 0.4% to 7.8%, so find out your own number
- The primary outcome was prevalence; the aspiration comparison is a secondary outcome and should be read as such
- 41% obtained the drug from neither a GP nor a hospital specialist: ask patients by drug name, at assessment and again on the day
- Tirzepatide 70%, semaglutide 26%; 97% on weekly injections; 59% taking it for weight loss
- Aspiration and/or regurgitation occurred in 1 in 71 of exposed patients against 1 in 802 of the unexposed, odds ratio 11.4 (95% CI 6.5–20)
- All 19 events in the exposed group involved regurgitation; only two were pulmonary aspiration, an aspiration rate of about 1 in 674, so the mitigation appears to have worked
- More than half the events occurred at emergence, not induction: the extubation plan matters at least as much as the intubation plan
- Stopping for a week is pharmacologically meaningless against half-lives of five and seven days; national consensus guidance recommends continuing
- Gastric ultrasound was used in 1.3% of patients: a skills gap, and an open goal for a specialty that already images the stomach
- This is an association from a study that could not adjust for confounding, in which observers knew exposure status: hold it firmly but not tightly
- For cardiac patients, emergence usually happens on the ICU hours later, which makes GLP-1 use a handover item
- Stopping the drug in cardiac surgical patients risks hyperglycaemia and its associated sternal wound infection, without reliably emptying the stomach
- GLIMPSE excluded patients whose airway management began outside the procedure location, so the critical care population is not represented

## Questions
::: qa
Q: A patient on semaglutide tells you they stopped it "a week ago, like the leaflet said". How much of the drug is left, and what does that mean for your plan?
A: Most of it. With a half-life of about seven days, one week leaves roughly half the steady-state level; five half-lives (around 35 days) would be needed to eliminate it. Plan as though they are still taking it.
R: The GLIMPSE cohort showed 30% of patients were asked to stop, mostly for 8–14 days, which is pharmacologically meaningless for weekly agents (semaglutide ~7 days, tirzepatide ~5 days). National consensus guidance recommends continuing the drug and managing aspiration risk instead, because stopping also risks glycaemic deterioration, which in cardiac surgery means sternal wound infection.

Q: Why should the GLIMPSE odds ratio of 11 for aspiration or regurgitation be read more cautiously than the prevalence figure of 1 in 36?
A: Because prevalence was the primary outcome the study was designed and powered for; the aspiration comparison was a secondary outcome from an observational cohort that could not adjust for confounding and in which observers knew exposure status.
R: Small event numbers (19 in the exposed group, only two true pulmonary aspirations) drive a large ratio with wide confidence intervals (6.5–20), and knowing a patient was on a GLP-1 agonist could plausibly both raise vigilance for regurgitation and change airway management. The NAP7 cross-check partly defends the finding, but it remains an association, not a causal estimate.

Q: Why does GLIMPSE's finding that most events occurred at emergence matter more for a cardiac unit than for a day-case list?
A: Because for cardiac surgical patients emergence usually happens on the intensive care unit hours after the anaesthetist has left, so the aspiration risk transfers to a different team at a different time. It becomes a handover item, not just an induction plan.
R: More than half the events in the exposed group happened at emergence rather than induction. On a cardiothoracic unit the person extubating is not the person who took the drug history, and the drug may not appear on any record if it was obtained privately (41% of cases), so the only way it reaches the unit is if the anaesthetist hands it over explicitly.
:::
