We've spent a lot of this series on machines: balloon pumps, ECMO circuits, ventricular assist devices. This is about the tool that makes all three of them safe.

The argument is that in mechanical circulatory support, transoesophageal echo is not an investigation you order. It is part of the device. It confirms the indication, guides the cannulas in, optimises the support once it is running, and finds the complication before the patient tells you about it. One rule runs through the whole topic: any deterioration in a patient on mechanical support gets a TOE, with specific attention to cannula malposition or occlusion. This is part one, covering balloon pumps and ECMO; part two takes on assist devices and the Impella. The structure follows the mechanical support chapter of the fourth edition of Practical Perioperative Transoesophageal Echocardiography.

## The balloon pump

TOE has real advantages over the chest film and fluoroscopy: real-time structures, portable, no ionising radiation. Scan before you insert, to confirm the indication and to exclude the absolute contraindications: severe aortic regurgitation, severe atheroma in the descending thoracic aorta, aortic dissection, and severe distal occlusive disease. Regurgitation is so absolute because the balloon inflates in diastole to augment diastolic pressure, so a leaking valve means you drive that augmented pressure straight back into the ventricle. The device fails at its own job. Then the relative contraindications, including dynamic LVOT obstruction, where reducing afterload makes systolic anterior motion worse.

Positioning: the tip about two centimetres distal to the origin of the left subclavian, with the proximal balloon above the diaphragm. Too high and you occlude the subclavian, too low and you lose augmentation and may intermittently occlude the mesenteric or renal arteries. How to find the subclavian origin in the upper oesophageal arch short axis, how to tell the echo-dense tip and lucent balloon body from the wire, and a technique that needs no technology at all: identify the balloon tip in the descending aorta short axis, put your fingertips on the probe at the level of the teeth, withdraw until the subclavian origin appears, and the distance your fingers have travelled is the distance from tip to subclavian.

## ECMO: choosing the mode

The most useful piece of thinking in the episode. When a severe respiratory failure patient becomes haemodynamically unstable, it is usually one of four things: right ventricular dysfunction from aggressive ventilation and acutely raised pulmonary vascular resistance, ventricular dysfunction from sepsis or underlying cardiac disease, vasodilation from sepsis, or hypovolaemia. Most of those do not need arterial support. Ventilator-induced RV dysfunction usually resolves once ECMO lets you drop to rest settings, and vasodilation is usually managed with VV plus vasopressors. Instability alone does not mean VA; it means work out the mechanism first, and echo is how you do that.

Before you commit: significant aortic or mitral regurgitation, which can cause acute LV distension in a VA patient who is barely ejecting; severe aortic disease as a contraindication to VA; severe atheroma as a relative one, because of the sandblasting effect of the return jet; and the precise position on a PFO, which may complicate VV but is not a contraindication.

## Cannulas, recirculation, and the complications that hide

Cannula positioning by configuration: confirming the guidewire before you dilate, where the return tip belongs in VV, where the drainage tip goes for jugular–femoral versus femoral–femoral versus a double-lumen cannula, and why VA is simpler. Recirculation: how to diagnose it with 2D and colour Doppler, the two findings that confirm it, and how to fix it under echo guidance.

Then the complications that are hard to see coming. The specific screen appearance of LV distension on VA and the two families of solution. Circuit and intracardiac thrombus. And tamponade after cardiac surgery, which is difficult because the circuit largely bypasses flow through the heart, so it is initially well tolerated and you never get the classic picture, until the right atrium collapses and your circuit flow falls. Raised central venous pressures with low circuit flows on central VA means tamponade until proven otherwise. Plus the TOE predictors associated with successful weaning from VA.

## Key takeaways
- In mechanical circulatory support, TOE is part of the device rather than an investigation you order
- Any deterioration in a patient on mechanical support gets a TOE, looking specifically for cannula malposition or occlusion
- Absolute contraindications to an IABP on echo: severe aortic regurgitation, severe descending aortic atheroma, dissection, and severe distal occlusive disease
- Aortic regurgitation is absolute because augmenting diastolic pressure across a leaking valve worsens the regurgitation without improving coronary perfusion pressure
- Reducing afterload worsens dynamic LVOT obstruction and systolic anterior motion, so that is a relative contraindication
- Position the balloon tip about 2 cm distal to the left subclavian origin, with the proximal balloon above the diaphragm
- You can measure tip-to-subclavian distance by walking the probe back with your fingers at the level of the teeth
- ECMO mode follows the mechanism: instability alone does not mean VA
- Ventilator-induced RV dysfunction and septic vasodilation are usually managed on VV, with rest settings and vasopressors
- Assess both the aortic and mitral valves before VA, because regurgitation predisposes to acute LV distension in a barely ejecting ventricle
- Severe aortic dissection or aneurysm contraindicates VA; severe atheroma is relative, because of the sandblasting effect of the return jet
- A PFO may complicate VV ECMO but is not a contraindication to ECMO
- Confirm the guidewire with the probe before dilating and passing a large-bore cannula
- In VV, the return tip sits in the right atrium with the jet aimed at the tricuspid valve, and the tips must be kept apart to avoid recirculation
- Diagnose recirculation by finding both tips on 2D, then looking for a high-velocity jet down the IVC or a jet that includes the drainage tip
- LV distension on VA looks like a distended ventricle, minimal contractility, a closed aortic valve, a distended left atrium and significant mitral regurgitation, with a non-pulsatile arterial trace
- Severe distension causes pulmonary oedema, delays recovery and predisposes to intracavity thrombus
- Tamponade is poorly detected on central VA because the circuit bypasses the heart: raised venous pressures with low circuit flows means tamponade until proven otherwise
- Have a low threshold for rescanning after starting ECMO: complications are common and hard to detect early

## Questions
::: qa
Q: A patient with severe ARDS on maximal ventilation becomes hypotensive with a lactate of 6. The referring team request VA-ECMO. What do you do before agreeing to the mode?
A: Echo the patient to identify the mechanism. Most instability in respiratory failure is ventilator-induced RV dysfunction, sepsis-related vasodilation, or hypovolaemia, all of which are managed on VV-ECMO with rest ventilation and vasopressors.
R: VA carries a higher complication burden (limb ischaemia, LV distension, Harlequin, stroke). Only primary ventricular failure that will not resolve with lung rest justifies arterial support. Instability alone does not mean VA.

Q: On VA-ECMO the LV looks distended, the aortic valve is not opening, and there is new severe mitral regurgitation with spontaneous echo contrast in the left atrium. What is happening and what are the two families of solution?
A: Left ventricular distension: the retrograde arterial return raises afterload beyond what a barely-ejecting ventricle can overcome. Solutions are either to reduce the load (lower circuit flow, inotropes, vasodilators, IABP) or to vent the ventricle directly (Impella, surgical LV vent, atrial septostomy).
R: Untreated distension causes pulmonary oedema, LV thrombus and delays myocardial recovery. Recognising the closed aortic valve and non-pulsatile arterial trace early is the point of scanning after starting support.

Q: Why is tamponade hard to detect on central VA-ECMO, and what is the clue?
A: Because the circuit drains the right atrium and returns to the aorta, so the heart is largely bypassed and the classic chamber collapse and hypotension appear late. The clue is rising central venous pressure with falling circuit flows.
R: The drainage cannula is the first thing the pericardial collection compromises. Raised venous pressures with low flows on central VA means tamponade until echo proves otherwise.
:::
