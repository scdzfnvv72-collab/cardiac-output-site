Two in the morning, day zero after cardiac surgery. The chest drains have been filling steadily for an hour, the nurse is looking at you, and the surgical registrar is on the phone. Your instinct is to send a TEG and wait for it to tell you what to do, and that instinct, the one we are all taught, may be exactly what harms this patient.

The intensive care half of the bleeding week: resternotomy, blood products, point-of-care testing and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. Mike takes a deliberately unfashionable position on the TEG and defends it, and Calum pushes back.

## Going back

Resternotomy for bleeding happens perhaps a couple of times a week, and once a patient needs a second procedure the odds of a poor outcome climb sharply: more red cells, more acute kidney injury and filtration, prolonged intubation, tracheostomy and a longer stay. Some of that is confounding, but the UK audit data is clear enough. Which makes prevention one of the real jobs of the cardiac anaesthetist: stop the dual antiplatelets a week or two beforehand, insist on a proper period of haemostasis at the end of the case, and don't let surgeons close wet chests. If they still aren't dry, encourage the surgeon to leave the chest open. That is a good decision, not a failure of the operation, and the conversation needs diplomacy.

## The products, in pairs

FFP is human plasma, so it contains everything, but it comes at high volume, and pushing four units in fast can cause real problems for the right ventricle. Octaplex is four-factor prothrombin complex, made up at the bedside, low volume, titratable, with around three thousand units to reverse warfarin or a DOAC, and a thousand units often enough to dry up needle-hole oozing that isn't responding to protamine. Then cryoprecipitate versus fibrinogen concentrate, where the newer, more expensive product isn't automatically the better one: cryo also carries von Willebrand factor and factor XIII, and in a patient with the acquired von Willebrand disorder that severe aortic stenosis gives you, the "inferior" product may be exactly the right one.

## The argument about point-of-care testing

Algorithms beat individual judgement and point-of-care testing produces more restrictive transfusion than laboratory testing, but it hasn't yet shown better outcomes, and in a patient with very high drain output the danger is waiting for information before doing anything to stop the bleeding. In a genuinely bleeding cardiac patient the TEG is usually hard to interpret and normally tells you to give everything. Use it, but never let it delay treatment, and have a low threshold for a TOE, because tamponade is the diagnosis you cannot afford to miss while you're chasing numbers.

## HIT

A rare, immune-mediated, severe drug reaction with antibodies against the platelet factor four–heparin complex, causing platelet activation, thrombin generation and life-threatening thrombosis: a clotting disease with a low platelet count, and around six per cent mortality per day untreated. The 4T score and why pre-test probability still matters when you're sending a test anyway; separating it from the far more common heparin-associated thrombocytopenia; the biphasic platelet pattern after cardiac surgery; why a count above 150 doesn't exclude it. The in-house immunoassay that rules out versus the functional assay that confirms, why platelet transfusion is contraindicated, what plasma exchange and IVIG buy you, and argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor that needs no cofactor. And a case where a patient was bridged straight onto warfarin instead, and thrombosed.

## Key takeaways
- Going back to theatre for bleeding is a serious marker of poor outcome: more transfusion, more AKI, longer ventilation and longer stay
- Prevention starts in theatre: stop dual antiplatelets, take a proper haemostatic pause, and never let a surgeon close a wet chest
- If they still aren't dry, leaving the chest open is a good decision rather than a failure
- FFP contains everything but at high volume; pushing it in fast can be very bad for the right ventricle
- Octaplex is four-factor PCC, made up at the bedside, low volume and titratable; around 3,000 units reverses warfarin or a DOAC
- A thousand units of Octaplex often settles needle-hole oozing that protamine hasn't fixed
- Cryoprecipitate isn't simply the inferior product: it carries von Willebrand factor and factor XIII, which may be exactly what an aortic stenosis patient needs
- Use the TEG, but never wait for it in the acutely bleeding patient: give products, tell the surgeon, and go back if you can't stop it
- Have a low threshold for TOE, specifically to exclude tamponade
- HIT is a prothrombotic disease with a low platelet count, and roughly 6% mortality per day untreated
- Use the 4T score for pre-test probability, look for the biphasic platelet pattern, and don't be reassured by a count above 150
- The in-house immunoassay rules out; the functional assay confirms; both can be falsely negative
- Platelet transfusion in acute HIT is contraindicated; plasma exchange and IVIG are what buy you heparin
- Stopping heparin is necessary but not sufficient: argatroban is a direct thrombin inhibitor needing no cofactor, and you must never bridge a HIT patient onto warfarin alone

## Questions
::: qa
Q: Platelets after CABG: 180 on day 1, 95 on day 2, 160 on day 5, 70 on day 8. What pattern is this and why does it matter?
A: A biphasic fall. The first drop is the expected dilution and consumption of bypass; the second, after recovery, is the signature of HIT and should prompt a 4T score and testing.
R: HIT antibodies typically appear 5–10 days after heparin exposure. A count of 70 is a 60% fall from the recovered baseline even though it's above the "abnormal" threshold that would worry a general ICU; the proportion matters more than the absolute number.

Q: Why is warfarin alone dangerous in acute HIT?
A: Warfarin lowers protein C before it lowers the procoagulant factors, worsening the prothrombotic state and precipitating venous limb gangrene or skin necrosis.
R: Anticoagulate with a non-heparin agent (argatroban, or bivalirudin) until platelets have recovered, then overlap with warfarin for at least five days. Never bridge straight to warfarin.

Q: Why might cryoprecipitate be the better choice over fibrinogen concentrate for a bleeding patient after AVR for severe aortic stenosis?
A: Because severe aortic stenosis causes acquired von Willebrand syndrome, and cryoprecipitate contains von Willebrand factor and factor XIII as well as fibrinogen; fibrinogen concentrate contains fibrinogen alone.
R: Shear across the stenotic valve cleaves the large vWF multimers. The purified product corrects only one of the deficits. Match the product to the physiology rather than defaulting to the newer one.
:::
