Forty minutes into an aortic valve replacement, the cross-clamp is on and the heart is arrested. Then a little electrical activity starts creeping back on the ECG. What do you do? Tell the surgeon, out loud, straight away, because that heart is waking up while it is still ischaemic. The anaesthetist is part of myocardial protection, even though the solution runs through the perfusionist's pump.

This is cardioplegia from the top end of the table. Solutions, doses and protocols are Wythenshawe-specific: take the principles and check your own unit's practice.

## Why it exists

The moment the aorta is clamped, the heart has no coronary flow, and a warm, beating, ischaemic heart runs out of energy in minutes. Cardioplegia makes planned ischaemia survivable in five ways: arrest, cool, empty, feed and buffer, and limit reperfusion injury. Arrest does most of the work.

## What's in the bag

Grouped the way examiners like it: an arresting agent, buffers, osmotic agents and substrate. High potassium produces a depolarised arrest by raising the resting membrane potential and inactivating fast sodium channels, which is not quite a resting membrane. The commonest solution is 4:1 blood cardioplegia, and blood adds oxygen carriage, buffering, free-radical scavenging and better microvascular flow, with less oedema. Warm versus cold is a real trade-off: cold maximises metabolic suppression, warm supports aerobic metabolism, and the hot shot before the clamp comes off prepares the heart for reperfusion.

## Delivery

Antegrade into the aortic root as standard, direct into the ostia, or down the vein grafts. Retrograde through the coronary sinus for aortic regurgitation, aortic and root surgery, and severe coronary disease, with the 40 mmHg limit on coronary sinus pressure, the reason retrograde protects the right ventricle less well, and how to confirm the cannula on TOE. And why antegrade into a regurgitant root distends the ventricle instead of perfusing it: watch the LV on TOE and say if it is getting bigger.

## The seven complications

Trauma, air, oedema, bleeding, difficulty restarting, electrolyte disturbance and inadequate protection. The last one shows up in a specific way: as a heart that comes off badly.

## Beyond Fellowship

del Nido, with its single long-acting dose, lidocaine and low calcium. Custodiol (HTK), which arrests the heart by taking the sodium away rather than adding potassium, a hyperpolarised arrest, and the price you pay in volume and dilution. And Laplace's law, which explains why a distended ventricle is dangerous and why we vent: during surgery, with a balloon pump, and on VA-ECMO.

We finish with the anaesthetist's checklist during the cross-clamp, and what to be ready for when it comes off.

## Key takeaways
- If electrical activity returns on the ECG during the cross-clamp, tell the surgeon straight away: the heart is working while ischaemic
- Cardioplegia protects the heart in five ways: arrest, cool, empty, deliver substrate and buffering, and limit reperfusion injury. Arrest does the heavy lifting
- Contents fall into four groups: an arresting agent (usually potassium, with magnesium), buffers, osmotic agents and substrate
- High potassium raises the resting membrane potential and inactivates fast sodium channels: a depolarised arrest in diastole
- Blood cardioplegia (4:1) adds oxygen carriage, buffering, free-radical scavenging and better microvascular flow, with less oedema
- Cold maximises metabolic suppression; warm supports aerobic metabolism; a warm "hot shot" before clamp removal prepares the heart for reperfusion
- Retrograde delivery via the coronary sinus is used for significant aortic regurgitation, aortic valve and root surgery, and severe coronary disease: keep sinus pressure below 40 mmHg
- Retrograde protects the right ventricle less well, so it's often combined with antegrade
- In aortic regurgitation, antegrade root delivery distends the left ventricle: watch the LV on TOE and say if it's getting bigger
- Complications: trauma, air, myocardial oedema, bleeding, difficulty restarting, systemic electrolyte disturbance and inadequate protection
- del Nido (1 part blood to 4 crystalloid, with lidocaine and low calcium) gives a single dose lasting up to around 90 minutes
- Custodiol (HTK) arrests by sodium depletion, a hyperpolarised arrest, with long protection, but large volumes cause haemodilution and hyponatraemia
- Laplace: wall tension rises with pressure and radius, which is why distension is dangerous and why we vent
- Solutions, doses and protocols are Wythenshawe-specific: check your own unit's practice

## Questions
::: qa
Q: Explain how a high-potassium cardioplegia solution arrests the heart, and in which phase of the cycle.
A: Raising extracellular potassium depolarises the resting membrane potential from around −90 mV towards −50 mV, at which the fast voltage-gated sodium channels are inactivated and cannot recover, so no action potential upstroke can be generated. The heart arrests in diastole.
R: This is a depolarised arrest, distinct from the hyperpolarised arrest produced by sodium-depleting solutions such as Custodiol. A depolarised membrane is not a true resting state: there is continued ion flux and calcium loading over time, which is one reason conventional potassium cardioplegia has to be re-dosed every 20–30 minutes.

Q: The surgeon is repairing a root in a patient with severe aortic regurgitation. Why is antegrade cardioplegia into the aortic root unreliable, and what is done instead?
A: With an incompetent aortic valve, solution delivered into the root runs back through the valve into the left ventricle rather than down the coronary ostia, distending the ventricle without protecting the myocardium. Retrograde delivery through a coronary sinus cannula, direct ostial delivery, or both, is used instead.
R: Retrograde delivery has its own limits: coronary sinus pressure is kept below about 40 mmHg to avoid sinus injury, and it protects the right ventricle less well because the RV drains largely via Thebesian veins and the anterior cardiac veins, so it is commonly combined with antegrade or ostial doses. The anaesthetist confirms the retrograde cannula position on TOE and watches for LV distension throughout.

Q: Why does Laplace's law explain venting on bypass, the balloon pump and LV decompression on VA-ECMO?
A: Because wall tension is proportional to the product of intracavity pressure and radius; a distended, non-ejecting ventricle has high tension and therefore high oxygen demand at exactly the moment its supply is lowest. Reducing volume or pressure lowers tension, so every unloading strategy is a Laplace strategy.
R: On bypass with the clamp on, a distended LV from bronchial or Thebesian return, or from aortic regurgitation, raises subendocardial demand and stretches the myocardium, so it is vented. On VA-ECMO a poorly ejecting LV against retrograde aortic flow distends the same way, hence Impella or septostomy. The balloon pump reduces afterload and therefore end-systolic pressure and radius.
:::
