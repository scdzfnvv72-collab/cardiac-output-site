Your noradrenaline is climbing, and climbing, and the pressure still won't hold. The tank's the right size and the pump is working. So what do you reach for next?

This is the theatre half of the cardiac topic: the problems that show up on the table rather than on the unit, and the ones that catch people out.

## Vasoplegia

When to add vasopressin, why a femoral arterial line earns its place (the radiofemoral difference tracks the severity, and it takes days rather than hours to close), and the second-line agents: methylene blue at 1 mg/kg, why you warn the surgeon before you give it, and high-dose hydroxocobalamin when that isn't enough. Then a tour of the inotropes, and the honest admission that there is no perfect one and essentially no evidence on which is best in acute heart failure.

## The failing right ventricle, and SAM

Coming off bypass with a failing RV: get the rate up, get atrial wires in, nitric on, inotropes running, and choose Octaplex and fibrinogen over big volumes of FFP and cryo, because bleeding in the face of RV failure is one of the hardest balancing acts in the building. Systolic anterior motion is easy to miss, hard to manage, and can have you telling a surgeon there's a problem with a valve that's perfectly fine.

## Pacing, from the wires up

Why you nag for atrial wires, how to read the three-letter code, what AAI, DDD, VVI and the asynchronous modes actually do, and when diathermy forces your hand. Then the two dials everyone muddles, output and sensitivity, and the counterintuitive trap at the heart of it: turning the millivolt number up makes the box less sensitive, which is how you end up pacing onto a T wave. R-on-T is the commonest cause of cardiac arrest on a cardiac ICU, and NAP7 says so.

## Key takeaways
- Escalate vasoplegia in order: noradrenaline → vasopressin → methylene blue → hydroxocobalamin
- A femoral arterial line earns its place: the radiofemoral difference tracks the degree of vasoplegia
- There is no perfect inotrope, and almost no evidence on which is best in acute heart failure
- A failing RV coming off bypass wants rate, atrial wires, nitric and inotropes, and low-volume factor concentrates rather than FFP and cryo
- SAM is dynamic LVOT obstruction: manage it by filling and pacing, not by blaming the valve
- Nag for atrial wires: no atrial wire means you're stuck in VVI
- Set output at 2–3× the capture threshold, and re-check daily as the wires fibrose
- Sensitivity is inverted: a higher millivolt setting makes the box less sensitive; undersensing causes R-on-T, the commonest cause of arrest on a cardiac ICU (NAP7)

## Questions
::: qa
Q: The pacing box sensitivity is set at 8 mV in VVI mode. The patient has an intrinsic rhythm at 70. What is the risk?
A: Undersensing. The box may not see the patient's own QRS and will deliver a paced beat on the T wave, risking R-on-T and ventricular fibrillation.
R: Sensitivity is the threshold a signal must exceed to be detected. Raising the number raises the threshold, so the box becomes less sensitive. Set sensitivity low (around half the sensed R-wave amplitude) so intrinsic beats inhibit pacing, and check it daily.

Q: After mitral repair the patient is hypotensive with a new gradient across the LVOT and mitral regurgitation on TOE. The surgeon asks whether the repair has failed. What do you say?
A: This is probably SAM (systolic anterior motion), not a failed repair. Fill the ventricle, slow the heart, stop inotropes and raise the afterload, then rescan.
R: A small, underfilled, hyperdynamic LV with a repaired mitral valve drags the anterior leaflet into the outflow tract. Inotropes and vasodilation worsen it. Most SAM resolves with volume, beta-blockade and vasoconstriction; only persistent SAM needs surgical revision.

Q: Why warn the surgeon before giving methylene blue?
A: Because it turns the blood, urine and surgical field a striking blue-green and transiently lowers pulse oximetry readings, which alarms everyone who wasn't expecting it.
R: The oximetry artefact is optical, not real desaturation. It also has serotonergic interactions (avoid with SSRIs where possible) and must not be given to a patient in a low-output state, where raising afterload is harmful.
:::
