The phone goes at eleven at night. There's a donor heart, and it's coming here. In a few hours that organ is going into a patient who is, right now, sitting on a ward waiting for you.

This is heart and lung transplantation from the listing call to the handover on the unit: the twelve hours in the middle. The postoperative ICU episode is separate. Drugs and doses are Wythenshawe's local protocol, given as a worked example.

## Before theatre

Candidacy is a gift of life, and the patients being transplanted now are sicker and more borderline than a decade ago; compliance and mental health belong in that conversation. Then the middle-of-the-night listing assessment: what the reg is actually there to check, and what's already been done.

## Preparation and lines

Products in the fridge, six units of red cells from the start, pacemaker to fixed mode, immunosuppression before they leave the ward. Induction drugs, including the vitamin K that stops the rebound phenomenon in a warfarinised LVAD explant. Then the lines: why the femoral venous sheath is an escape route for a balloon pump wire, why the right-sided line is shorter to dodge the caval snare, and the Swan you must remember to withdraw before bicaval cannulation.

## Lungs

Risk stratification up to "extreme high risk", where you cannulate the groin before you induce, with a primed circuit and two consultants plus an ECMO consultant scrubbed. Lung isolation, and the elegant argument for VA-ECMO over full bypass. The first implant: inflating to 15–20 cmH₂O, loosening the PA clamp, the three causes of hypotension at that moment, and the deliberately austere protective strategy for a new lung (FiO₂ 0.21, 3–4 mL/kg, under 20 cmH₂O), because hyperoxia drives primary graft dysfunction.

## Coming off

The written escalation ladder: milrinone, dopamine and noradrenaline, then sequential pacing, then the balloon pump, then nitric at 20 ppm, then conversion to VA-ECMO. You do not re-heparinise. Vasoplegia and its most dangerous trap: give methylene blue to a patient who is actually in a low cardiac output state and you have put a brick wall in front of the heart. Then the TOE numbers for the pulmonary vein and PA anastomoses, and an end-of-case checklist that includes putting the vascath in yourself.

## Key takeaways
- A transplant is a gift of life: candidacy is an MDT decision, not a 3am one
- Build your escape routes at the start: a femoral sheath that will take a balloon pump wire, a short right-sided line to clear the caval snare, and a Swan withdrawn before bicaval cannulation
- For the sickest lungs, cannulate the groin before you induce, with the room already full of the right people
- VA-ECMO beats bypass for lung transplant: less anticoagulation, less bleeding, and the heart keeps beating
- Protect the new lung (room air, 3–4 mL/kg, under 20 cmH₂O) because hyperoxia drives primary graft dysfunction
- Coming off, follow the ladder: inotropes → pacing → IABP → nitric → VA-ECMO, and don't re-heparinise
- Be certain it's vasoplegia before giving methylene blue; in a low output state it is a brick wall in front of the heart
- Put the vascath in yourself in theatre rather than leaving it to the unit

## Questions
::: qa
Q: Why is the pulmonary artery catheter withdrawn before bicaval cannulation in a heart transplant?
A: So the surgeon doesn't snare or transect it when the venae cavae are cannulated and the native heart is excised.
R: A catheter across the right atrium at explant is either sutured into the anastomosis or cut. Pull it back into the sheath (or the SVC) before cannulation and re-float it into the new heart after implantation.

Q: The first lung is in, the PA clamp comes off and the pressure drops. Give three causes.
A: Reperfusion of the new lung (vasodilation and release of cold, acidotic, potassium-rich effluent), acute right ventricular strain as the RV faces a new vascular bed, and hypovolaemia or air.
R: Anticipate it: warn the surgeon, have vasopressor drawn up, ventilate the new lung gently, and check the RV and the anastomoses on TOE rather than reflexively giving volume.

Q: Why is FiO₂ 0.21 used on a freshly implanted lung?
A: Hyperoxia contributes to ischaemia–reperfusion injury and primary graft dysfunction; a lung that has been cold-stored and reperfused is at its most vulnerable to oxidative stress.
R: Combine low FiO₂ with small tidal volumes (3–4 mL/kg to the new lung) and pressures under 20 cmH₂O; titrate oxygen only to acceptable saturations rather than to a normal PaO₂.
:::
